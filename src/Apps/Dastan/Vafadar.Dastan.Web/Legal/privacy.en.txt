# 3. When you open a page

## What is processed

To send you a page, the server must process what your browser sends with every request: the IP address of your connection, the date and time, the address of the page, the page you came from where your browser says so, and the kind and language of your browser.

The program of the site itself writes none of this into a file or a database. It uses the IP address in memory only, for two things: to answer the request, and to count for one hour how often a connection has tried to register, to sign in, or to send a report or a notice, so that nobody can flood the site or guess passwords. The count is not stored and is gone after the hour or when the site restarts.

Delivering the pages you ask for, and keeping the site safe and working. The legal ground is Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR: our legitimate interest in running a site that is safe and available. Without this processing no page could reach you. For signed-in pages the ground is also Article 6 (1) (b) GDPR (performing the contract of use).

## How long

The site keeps nothing of a request once it is answered, except the count in memory described above (one hour at most).

# 4. What the site stores on your device

The site puts small pieces of information on your device (cookies) only where the function you use needs them: the language and the colours you chose, how far you have read, your saved list, your sign-in, and the protection of forms. This needs no consent under § 25 (2) no. 2 of the German Telecommunications Digital Services Data Protection Act (TDDDG), which implements Article 5 (3) of Directive 2002/58/EC. Pages are kept on your device for reading without a connection only when you turned that on (consent, § 25 (1) TDDDG); you can turn it off at any time.

Every item, with its name, purpose and lifetime, is listed at {{site}}/en/cookies. There are no cookies of other companies, none for advertising and none for statistics.

# 5. An account

## What is processed

- The name you choose. It need not be your real name. Somebody who has an account and knows the name can ask you for a relation; the people you are related to, share a tree with or are tagged with see it.

- When the account was made; which version of the terms of use and of this notice you confirmed, and when.
- How many wrong passwords were given in a row and, after five, until when sign-in is paused (15 minutes).
- When the account was closed, if it was, and by whom (you or the site).
- A random value that signs the account out everywhere when the password changes.
- On your device: a cookie that keeps you signed in for up to 30 days.

## Purpose and legal ground

Giving you the account and the private area, keeping them safe, and showing that you agreed to the terms. Legal ground: Article 6 (1) (b) GDPR (performing the contract of use you enter into when you register); for the protection against guessed passwords and for the record of your agreement also Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR (legitimate interest in security and in being able to show what was agreed).

As long as the account exists. When you close it, it disappears from the site at once and you are signed out everywhere; for 30 days you can open it again by signing in. After 30 days the account and everything that belongs to it is deleted for good (section 12). An account closed by the site is kept and deleted in the same way. A mail we send you is kept until it has been handed over for sending; then its text is emptied, and the record that it was sent (to which address, when, of which kind) is deleted after 30 days. A mail that cannot be handed over is tried again for about two days and then given up.

## Your e-mail address and the mail we send

We send mail only about your account: the link that confirms your address; a link to set a new password when you ask for one; a note to the old and to the new address when the address is changed; a note when the password or the way of signing in changes; and, when we close or restrict your account or something of yours, the decision with its reasons. If another user asks you for a relation, tags you on a photograph or asks for your agreement, we tell you so by mail, without saying what it is about; you can turn these messages off on the page of your account. We send no newsletter and no advertising. A link in a mail leads to this site and nowhere else, and opening it changes nothing until you press the button on the page it leads to. If you lose the password, a mailed link or a recovery code lets you set a new one. Whether an address has an account is told to nobody but the person who has the address. Legal ground: Article 6 (1) (b) GDPR; for the messages about what other users ask of you, Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR (the interest in your knowing what concerns you), to which you object by turning them off.

# 6. Signing in through Google or Microsoft

You can sign in through an account you have at Google or at Microsoft instead of a password. Nothing is loaded from these companies and nothing is sent to them until you press the button.

## What happens when you press the button

Your browser goes to the sign-in page of the company. There you sign in and the company tells this site that it is you. The company thereby learns that you use this site, when you sign in to it, and the technical data of your browser. The site asks for two things: an identifier that stands for your account there, and the e-mail address the company has confirmed for you. From Google it asks with the scopes "openid" and "email" and takes the address only when Google says that it has verified it. With an address Google has verified, your first sign-in makes an account with that address at once. From Microsoft the site takes the address of your mailbox or, where there is none, the address you sign in with, only as a proposal for the form: Microsoft does not say that such an address is yours. You write the address yourself, the site mails a link to it, and the account exists once you have opened that link; until then nobody is signed in, also not through Microsoft. Everything else a company sends (such as the name) is dropped at once, before anything is stored. The site never asks for your picture, your contacts or access to your files or mail.

## What the site keeps

Which company it was and a hash of that identifier, so that it recognises you the next time, and the e-mail address as the address of your account (section 5). During the sign-in two short-lived cookies are set (see the cookies page).

# 8. Reports and notices

## Reporting a mistake on an editorial page

Every statement of an editorial page can be reported as wrong. The site keeps the day, the page and statement, the language and what you wrote – no name, no IP address and no account. Please write nothing about yourself or other people into the text. Legal ground: Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR (our interest in correct content). A report is deleted 90 days after an editor looked at it.

## Reporting content (notice)

With the form at {{site}}/en/notice anybody can tell us that something on the site is against the law or the terms. The site keeps the time, the address of the content, the kind of problem, your explanation, and – only if you give them – your name and e-mail address. We use this to examine the notice, to decide, to answer you, and to be able to show how we dealt with it. Whoever stored the content is told the reasons for a decision, but not who gave the notice, unless that is necessary and lawful.

Legal ground: Article 6 (1) (c) GDPR together with Article 16 of Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, where that regulation applies to us, and otherwise Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR (our interest in removing illegal content and in protecting the people concerned). A notice is deleted one year after we decided on it; we keep it longer only as long as a dispute about it lasts.

## Decisions about content and accounts

If we hide a photograph, take a family tree back from the public or close an account, we keep the decision with the account: what it concerns, the facts, the legal or contractual ground, whether it followed a notice, and the date. The person concerned reads it in the account. Legal ground: Article 6 (1) (c) GDPR together with Article 17 of Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, otherwise Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR. It is deleted with the account.

# 9. If somebody else put data about you on the site

This section is for people who are named in a family tree or shown on a photograph of a user – whether they have an account or not (information under Article 14 GDPR).

## Which data

In a family tree: a name, a year of birth, a year of death or the note that the person has died, and how the person is related to others in the tree. On a photograph: your likeness, the caption, the date, and – only if you have an account and were tagged – the link to your account. The site has no fields for religion, health, origin, political opinion or other especially protected data, and its terms forbid entering such data about living people.

## Where the data come from

From the user who made the tree or uploaded the photograph. The site does not collect data about you from anywhere else.

## Who sees them

A family tree is seen only by its owner and the accounts the owner shares it with. It becomes public only if every living person it names has an account and has agreed; a tree that names a living person who has no account cannot be made public at all. A photograph is seen by the people its owner chose. If you have an account and are tagged, the tag shows only after you confirmed it, and the photograph is shown to everybody only if every tagged person agreed.

## Purpose and legal ground

Users keep the history of their families and their memories, and share them with the people close to them. As far as a user does this only within the family and among friends, the user's own processing is a purely personal or family activity to which the GDPR does not apply (Article 2 (2) (c) GDPR). As far as we store and show these data for the user, the legal ground is Article 6 (1) (f) GDPR: the legitimate interest of the user, and of the family, in recording and sharing family history, which we weigh against your interests – this is why trees and photographs are private by default and can be public only with consent. Where you agreed to be named or shown publicly, the ground is your consent (Article 6 (1) (a) GDPR).

Together with the user who stored the data we can be jointly responsible for them. Whatever the arrangement between us and the user, you can turn to us with all your rights.

## Your right to object and to have data removed

You can object at any time, without giving special reasons, to being named in a tree or shown on a photograph on this site, and ask for the data to be removed (Articles 17 and 21 GDPR). Use the form at {{site}}/en/notice or write to {{operator.mail}}. We ask the user to remove the data and remove them ourselves if the user does not. If you have an account, you can remove a tag yourself, object to a photograph in your account, and take back your consent to a public tree; each takes effect at once.

## How long

Until the user removes the data or the account of the user is deleted, or until we remove them at your request.

## People who have died

Data protection law does not apply to people who have died. Their memory and the rights of their relatives are protected by other laws; if you think a record or a picture of a deceased relative violates them, tell us in the same way.
